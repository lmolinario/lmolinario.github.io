Research portfolio

Trustworthy AI for digital investigations starts with measurable failure modes.

My research focuses on a practical question: when AI is used in a forensic or investigative workflow, how do we know when its output is reliable enough to support a decision? The work therefore emphasizes robustness, traceability, reproducibility, evidence provenance and technical accountability rather than benchmark accuracy alone.

Current experimental research

The main active research line is FAIR-Lab, developed around my Master's thesis in Computer Engineering, Cybersecurity and Artificial Intelligence.

Research questions

The work is organized around questions that matter operationally, not only algorithmically.

Robustness

How does performance change when evidence is perturbed, recompressed, resized, blurred, colour-shifted or deliberately attacked?

Generalization

How do models behave on out-of-distribution material that is plausible in an investigation but absent from clean training assumptions?

Explainability

Can attribution methods help expose brittle decisions or misleading confidence in failure cases?

Operational risk

Which false negatives, false positives and OOD behaviours create the highest evidentiary or investigative risk?

Human validation

Where should automated output stop and qualified human review become mandatory?

Research trajectory

FAIR-Lab is the first layer of a broader research agenda on trustworthy AI for digital forensics. The areas below are presented as forward research directions, not as completed results.

Planned direction

Agentic AI for forensics

How autonomous or semi-autonomous AI agents can assist investigations while preserving human authority, evidence boundaries, provenance, auditability and explicit approval points.

Planned direction

Anti-AI forensics & authenticity

Methods for assessing whether multimedia evidence is authentic, manipulated or AI-generated, including provenance signals and uncertainty-aware conclusions.

Planned direction

Private / local forensic AI

On-premise and local-model architectures for sensitive forensic data where confidentiality, data control and reproducibility matter more than cloud convenience.

Planned direction

Technical accountability

Operational controls for documenting model version, input boundary, tool effects, uncertainty, validation and human decision responsibility.

What counts as evidence of progress

The research portfolio distinguishes claims from outputs. Public pages should point to methodology, measurable experiments, documented assumptions or reproducible artifacts whenever possible.

Method

Explicit datasets, perturbation families, model/tool conditions, evaluation metrics and human review criteria.

Results

Comparative robustness tables, confusion matrices, operational risk summaries and selected explainability cases.

Reproducibility

Frozen manifests, hashes, traceable subsets and methodological documentation rather than opaque one-off demonstrations.

Positioning: the central research theme is not “AI applied to forensics” in the generic sense. It is whether AI-assisted investigative technology can be made robust, verifiable, privacy-preserving and technically accountable enough for evidence-sensitive work.
